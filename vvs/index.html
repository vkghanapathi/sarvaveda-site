About this Series

Purpose

Why this Series matters

Rarely has the whole of the Sanātana Dharma tradition been laid out, in a single ordered series, in clear modern English — faithful to the primary sources, yet legible to a reader who has never opened a Sanskrit text. That is the gap the Veda Vijñāna Series exists to close. Four convictions drive the work.

1

Preserving an endangered intellectual heritage

For more than three thousand years the knowledge of the Vedas and their ancillary sciences was carried by an unbroken oral lineage of teacher and pupil. That lineage is now thinning: each generation, fewer scholars remain who can read the mula text, hold the accent, and expound the meaning.

The Series fixes this living inheritance in a durable, referenced form — in print and in permanent digital editions — so that no branch of the tradition is lost for want of a competent exposition in a language the modern reader commands.

2

Opening the tradition to the English-reading world

Most of the world's students of Dharma — in India and across the diaspora — now read most comfortably in English, yet the reliable expositions remain locked in Sanskrit or in academic monographs that presume years of prior training. The Series meets the reader where they are: a learner in Mysore, Melbourne, or Minneapolis can open any volume and find the tradition on its own terms — consistent IAST transliteration, genuine primary sources rather than second-hand summaries, and explanations pitched to be understood, not merely admired.

3

From sacred text to living classroom

Every volume is written to be taught. The prose is pitched at the serious high-school and undergraduate reader; technical terms are introduced, not assumed; and the material is structured for a syllabus, not only a shelf. A companion digital edition makes the same content searchable and portable, and a bank of more than fourteen hundred assessment questions turns the Series into a ready resource for examinations, quiz competitions, and guided self-study.

4

Scholarship without compromise

Accessibility is never bought at the price of accuracy. Each volume works from the primary texts; transliteration follows the international IAST convention throughout; Vedic accents and special characters are set in the dedicated VijayaDV font so that nothing is silently normalised or lost; and every completed draft passes through scholarly review before it is declared ready — a body of work a specialist can cite with confidence and a newcomer can read with ease.

Order

The architecture of the Series

The seventy-two volumes are not a miscellany. They follow the classical order in which a traditional institution imparts knowledge — outward from the root to its branches, and from revealed text to applied science and lived culture. Twelve thematic divisions organise the whole: the foundational śāstras establish the ground; the Veda corpus presents the four Vedas and their auxiliaries; the Kalpa Sūtra manuals and applied “Solutions” volumes carry the ritual into practice; the Vedāṅgas and Upāṅgas supply the six limbs and the schools of reasoning; the Upavedas open onto medicine, defence, music, and statecraft; Itihāsa and the Purāṇas preserve narrative and cosmology; Dharmaśāstra codifies conduct; literature, arts, and mathematics record the civilisational achievement; the Punya Kṣetra series maps sacred geography; and the closing volumes gather the lives of the Seers, Saints, and leaders who carried the tradition to today.

Readership

Whom the Series serves

The Series is written for six overlapping readerships, and each volume is measured against all of them:

The scholarwho requires reliable primary-source references, consistent transliteration, and citable expositions.
The studentin school, college, or a traditional paṭhaśālā, who needs a structured, teachable text and a means of self-assessment.
The practising Vaidikawho seeks clear, applied guidance for ritual and observance drawn directly from the manuals.
The institution and librarywhich needs an authoritative, complete, and well-produced reference collection.
The diaspora familywhich wishes to pass the tradition to children who read and think in English.
The general readerof serious interest, who wants the tradition explained honestly and whole.
Production

How each volume is made

Every title advances through the same disciplined pipeline: a detailed structural blueprint is drafted; the text is composed from the mula sources with full IAST transliteration and Vedic-accent typesetting; the completed draft is examined and corrected by qualified reviewers; Sanskrit and English are aligned for study editions where relevant; and the volume is finally issued in two forms — an archival print edition and a permanent online edition. Each volume's position in this pipeline is recorded as its production status in the register that follows.

FOUNDATIONAL ŚĀSTRASVols. 1–10

1

The Veda

Ready

A guided textbook that opens the whole Vedic corpus for the newcomer: what the Veda is, its division into Mantra and Brāhmaṇa and into karma- and jñāna-kāṇḍa, and why it exists. Built on the discourses of the Kāñcī Paramācārya and other teachers of our own time, with a careful key to IAST pronunciation and the svaras.

2

Vedānta — Upaniṣad

Ready

The second guided textbook carries the reader from ritual to realisation, presenting the Upaniṣads as the culmination — the anta — of the Veda. It surveys the principal Upaniṣadic teachings and the schools of Vedānta in the same accessible, teachable format as Book 1.

3

Vedāṅga — Upāṅga

Ready

The third foundational textbook maps the six limbs of the Veda — Śikṣā, Vyākaraṇa, Chandas, Nirukta, Kalpa, and Jyotiṣa — together with the ancillary sciences, showing how each protects and applies the revealed text. It prepares the student for the specialist volumes that follow.

4

Karma Prabodha 1 — Nitya Karma

For Review

An English rendering of Dr. Ghanapathi's teaching on the daily obligatory rites (nitya-karma), explaining why the Veda commands action and how the karma- and jñāna-kāṇḍas relate. As Part 1 of Book 4, it grounds ritual practice in its scriptural rationale.

5

Karma Prabodha 2 — Aitareya Brāhmaṇa

For Review

A comparative study of the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa and Āraṇyaka of the Ṛgveda, opening with the classical definitions of mantra, ṛk, sāman, and yajus and the categories of arthavāda. It treats Pañcikā I in full, Pañcikā II in part, and the remaining pañcikās through selected samples.

6

Karma Prabodha 3 — Pravargya Upasat Compendium

For Review

A study of the principles of the Pravargya and Upasad rites of the Kṛṣṇa-Yajurveda, examining the myth of the severed head of the yajña, the meaning of the śānti-mantras, and whether the Pravargya stands alone or is a limb of the Soma sacrifice.

7

Karma Prabodha 4 — Havir-Yajña (Kāṇva) 1

For Review

The first of a two-volume critical study of the havir-yajñas in the Kāṇva recension of the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa. It covers the preface, the establishing of the fires (Agnyādheya), and the iṣṭi rites through the darśa-pūrṇamāsa.

8

Karma Prabodha 4 — Havir-Yajña (Kāṇva) 2

For Review

The concluding volume carries the study through the Cāturmāsya seasonal rites and the paśubandha, with the technical definitions and the closing grantha-upasaṃhāra. Together the two volumes translate the complete thesis.

9

Karma Prabodha 5 — Agniṣṭoma Prayoga

Prior Production

A practical manual for the Agniṣṭoma, the archetypal one-day Soma sacrifice that serves as the model for the whole class of soma-yāgas. It documents the sequence of the rite, the offices of its priests, and its ritual application.

10

Karma Prabodha 6 — Agnicayana Prayoga

Prior Production

A manual for the Agnicayana, the grand rite of building the fire-altar of ten thousand bricks in the form of the bird — among the most elaborate of the śrauta sacrifices. It documents the construction, the mantras, and the symbolism of the rite.

A thematic anthology of thirty-five Sāmavedic stories in full narrative, each with its arthavāda frame, its connection to song, and its distilled teaching (upadeśa-sāra). The themes range from brahman-recognition to the cosmic power of gāna and the women who taught it.

A reference annexe that tabulates the stotra sequences of the Soma rites day by day — from the one-day Agniṣṭoma to the year-long sattras — with rite profiles, stoma progressions, and the climactic Mahāvrata day.

Atharvaveda

Prior Production

Presents the fourth Veda in its own right: its unique position in the canon, the seers Atharvan, Bhṛgu, and Āṅgiras, the Śaunakīya and Paippalāda recensions, and the five pillars of its content. It emphasises the Atharvaveda as medicine — the healing of the world.

A companion anthology of thirty-five Atharvavedic stories with their arthavāda frames and teachings, organised by theme — the healing triad of maṇi, mantra, and oṣadhi; the earth as healer; and philosophical liberation.

It bridges Vedic text and contemporary drug-delivery science.

Punya Kṣetra 3 — Gaṇeśa, Subrahmaṇya, Sūrya, Famous Devotees

Prior Production

The auspicious beginner Gaṇeśa and the Aṣṭa-Vināyaka circuit, the divine commander Subrahmaṇya and the six abodes of Murugan, and the solar deity Sūrya — their theology, iconography, sacred kṣetras, and famous devotees.

65

Punya Kṣetra 4 — Teertha Kshetras, Pilgrimage, Shraddha, Parikrama

Prior Production

The theology of the sacred crossing-place and the great tīrthas — rivers, sacred lakes, and ocean sites — and the major pilgrimage circuits such as the Char Dhāma, together with the observances of śrāddha and parikramā.

66

Attire, Adornment, Śikhā, Gṛha Vāstu, and the Material Culture of Bhārat

Prior Production

The aesthetic of daily life: the unstitched garments and their symbolism, the sixteen adornments (śṛṅgāra), the sacred śikhā, the nine gems and ornaments, and the planning of the home by the Vāstupuruṣa maṇḍala.

67

Cuisine of Bhārat, Mulika, Siddha Oṣadhi, Home Remedies

Prior Production

Food as life and medicine: the six tastes and the rule of suitability, the regional vegetarian cuisines, the structure and etiquette of the meal, spices as the pharmacy of the kitchen, and fasting, festival, and Siddha home remedies.

SEERS, SAINTS, AND CIVILISATION OF BHĀRATVols. 68–72

68

Seers and Sages — Part 1 (Purāṇa Kāla)

Prior Production

The ṛṣis of the Purāṇic age: their definition and cosmic function, the Vedic and Upaniṣadic seers, the Saptarṣis, and the great figures — Vyāsa, Nārada, Vasiṣṭha, Viśvāmitra, Agastya — together with the women seers of the tradition.

69

Dharmik Leaders — Part 2 (Known History)

Prior Production

The teachers of recorded history, from the śramaṇa challenge and Ādi Śaṅkara through the Vaiṣṇava ācāryas, the Bhakti movement, and the Sikh Gurus, to the modern reformers from Dayānanda and Vivekānanda to Gāndhī and Aurobindo.

70

Saints of Bhārat (Post-Independence, Global)

Prior Production

The dharmic teachers of the modern global era — from Śivānanda and Yogānanda through Prabhupāda, the yoga masters Iyengar and Jois, and other contemporary figures who carried the tradition worldwide.

71

Monarchy & Political Leaders of Bhārata

Prior Production

The theology of kingship (rājadharma) and the great dynasties — the solar and lunar lineages, the ideal king Rāma, and the historical empires from the Mauryas and Guptas through Vijayanagara — as the political expression of dharma.

72

Contributions of Bhārat to the World

Prior Production

A closing survey of Bhārat's gifts to civilisation: the decimal place-value system and zero, astronomy, Āyurveda, the six darśanas, Pāṇinian linguistics, the arts and architecture, and metallurgy and materials science.
